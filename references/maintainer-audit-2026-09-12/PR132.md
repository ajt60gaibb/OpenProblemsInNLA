### 1. Reduction and spectral curve: §1 and Lemma 1, equations (1)–(3)

For generic D, DD^T has distinct nonzero eigenvalues. Its eigenbasis is orthogonal and nonisotropic, so left/right complex orthogonal transformations reduce D to diagonal form while fixing the first coordinate in each factor. These transformations preserve both the bilinear squared distance and x_11=0. This is a generic reduction, not an assumption of real data or Hermitian SVD. Generic data statements transfer back from the diagonal slice because the orthogonal-action parametrization dominates the open set of such D; bad critical behavior is invariant under that action.

Independently eliminated the lower coordinates in the symmetric linearization. If t^2=lambda, the lower left/right coordinates satisfy

`u_i=(t c_i v_0+d_i b_i u_0)/(lambda-d_i^2)` and
`v_i=(d_i c_i v_0+t b_i u_0)/(lambda-d_i^2)`.

The remaining two equations give exactly the determinant in Lemma 1. Multiplication by f, with its monic normalization, yields `P=h-2gz-fz^2`. At lambda=d_i^2 the numerator `lambda F_b F_c-g^2` vanishes, so h is a polynomial; its leading degree is m+1. This verifies the completed square `fP=lambda F_bF_c-(fz+g)^2`. Squarefreeness of the branch polynomial makes the curve smooth where f is nonzero, and `P_z=-2g !=0` handles each f-root.

### 2. Complete critical-point correspondence: Lemma 2, equations (5)–(7)

The restricted determinant is a nonzero multiaffine polynomial. A repeated nonconstant factor would force degree at least two in some variable, so it is reduced. Thus its smooth locus consists of rank n-1 points where the full determinant gradient is independent of E_11; no lower-rank component is silently counted as smooth.

At a critical X, its normal decomposition is `U-X=t E_11+s u v^T`, with u and v respectively the left and right kernel vectors. A zero s would require A(0) singular, contradicting the nonzero singular-completion parameter. If u were isotropic, then `A(z)^T u=0`, hence z=z_* and u would be the nonisotropic one-dimensional left kernel specified by the generic conditions. The right-kernel argument is identical. Consequently both can be normalized, giving lambda=s^2 !=0.

The eigenvalue simplicity argument also survives complex symmetric, possibly defective matrices. A nonisotropic eigenvector splits off an orthogonal invariant line. If its eigenvalue had further algebraic multiplicity, the complement would contain another eigenvector, forcing adj(lambda I-AA^T)=0 and hence both partial derivatives of P to vanish. This contradicts the already established smoothness of P=0.

The derivative of a simple eigenvalue is `lambda'=2(Pi A)_11`; therefore x_11=0 is exactly `E=P_z/2+zP_lambda=0`. Conversely, every common solution has f!=0 and z!=z_* by the generic exclusions. Smoothness plus E=0 gives P_lambda!=0, and lambda=0 cannot satisfy E=0. Thus A is invertible, its spectral projector is regular, and `X=(I-Pi)A` has rank n-1 and x_11=0. If the gradient at X were proportional to E_11, both kernels would be e_1, so Pi A would be supported only at (1,1) and the first off-diagonal row b would vanish. This is excluded. The residual is the correct rank-one normal, establishing the reverse implication.

There is no sign/square-root doubling. The projector determines exactly one X. In the opposite direction, independence of E_11 and the determinant gradient gives a unique normal decomposition and hence z. Lambda is recovered as the squared bilinear Frobenius norm of the rank-one residual. These descriptions also provide regular local inverses on the stated open patches, not just a set-theoretic bijection.

### 3. Eliminant and discarded loci: §4, equations (8)–(11)

The resultant factor is **f^-2**, not f^-4 or an arbitrary saturation: replacing a cubic by its linear remainder contributes (-f)^2, while evaluating two denominators f^2 contributes f^-4. This proves equation (10) as the original Sylvester polynomial identity, including points at which direct division by f is unavailable.

Writing h=lambda f+r cancels the leading terms of alpha, giving degree at most 3m-2. The leading coefficient of beta is `[-1-2(m-1)+2m]G=G` in degree 3m-1. Thus `-f beta^2` alone has top degree 7m-2 and coefficient -G^2; the two other numerator terms have degrees at most 7m-3 and 7m-4. Division by monic f^2 gives exactly degree 5m-2. No degree upper bound is being mistaken for equality.

At a simple f-root, the quadratic P homogenized in z has an infinite root, but the homogenized cubic E evaluates there to -f', which is nonzero. Therefore the degree-drop specialization cannot create an automatic resultant zero at infinity. The finite point over that f-root is separately excluded by the witness. At other finite lambda, P remains genuinely quadratic, so ordinary resultant vanishing is equivalent to a finite common solution. Lambda=0 and z=0 are separately excluded. ### 5. All-dimension genericity witness: §5, especially equation (12)

The positive d_i, positive vector beta, r>0 with r!=1, and sufficiently small positive epsilon simultaneously establish every listed condition for each m>=2:

- The rank-one secular functions for F_b and F_c have simple positive interlacing roots. Their root sets are disjoint because a common root would require r^2=1. They do not vanish at the poles. Thus lambda F_b F_c is squarefree.
- G and the values g(d_i^2), g(0) are nonzero. The m-1 roots of g/epsilon^2 are fixed strictly between the poles. Meanwhile h's roots converge to 0 and those poles, proving gcd(g,h)=1 for small epsilon.
- At each f-root the finite z root tends to `-(1+r^2)d_i/(2r)`. Its magnitude strictly exceeds d_i, so the limit `q_i z(d_i^2-z^2)` for E is nonzero. There are finitely many such exclusions in each dimension, so one sufficiently small epsilon works for all.
- The only singular completion has z_*>0 and real one-dimensional left/right kernels with positive squared norms. Its zero spectral root cannot solve E=0.
- For each nonzero spectral root, the first left-singular coordinate is `epsilon beta_i/d_i+O(epsilon^3)`, while the corresponding first coordinate of A^T u_i is `r epsilon beta_i+O(epsilon^3)`. These expansions follow from the off-diagonal Gram entry `epsilon d_i beta_i+O(epsilon^3)` and the simple limiting eigenvalue d_i^2. Their product gives the stated residual entry. Subtracting it from z_* leaves `r epsilon^2 sum_(j!=i) beta_j^2/d_j+O(epsilon^4)>0`. This last positive sum is exactly where m>=2 is needed.

Failure conditions are algebraic after denominators are cleared; the constructed real witness therefore proves a nonempty complex algebraic open set, not merely a positive-probability numerical event. Nothing requires an epsilon choice uniform across all n: the theorem computes each dimension's generic degree separately.
